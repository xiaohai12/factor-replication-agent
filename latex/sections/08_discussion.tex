\section{Discussion, Limitations and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Implications for the factor zoo debate}

If, across this paper's sample, HXZ-versus-C\&Z divergence is systematically
attributable via $\Aref$ to a specific side's departure from what the paper
states -- rather than to the underlying factor being invalid -- then a
non-trivial share of what the literature records as replication failure is
better described as implementation disagreement misfiled as factor failure.
The converse is equally informative: factors where the paper is silent on a
field, yet $\Cagent$, $\Ccz$, and $\Cstd$ still land close together, are
the cases where a lack of specificity in the original text turned out not
to matter -- arguably the more genuinely robust anomalies, even though they
are rarely singled out as such. Both readings are grounded directly in the
outcome classification of \S\ref{sec:results}, not asserted independently
of it.

\subsection{Limitations}

\subsubsection{Methodological}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No within-agent dispersion study.} The variance of repeated
    extraction ($K \geq 5$ draws per paper) is unmeasured -- threatens C1:
    $\Acz - \Aref$ mixes LLM sampling noise with genuine paper ambiguity
    (the "overstatement" side of the two-sided bias, \S\ref{sec:scope}).
  \item \textbf{No Q2 bridge track.} The C\&Z reference signal has not been
    run through this engine -- blocks (a) cross-validating the engine's
    absolute output against C\&Z's own published AltPorts numbers, and
    (b) further splitting $(\CZ - \Acz)$ into signal-difference vs.\
    environment/vintage-difference; it is reported as one combined residual.
  \item \textbf{Human-confirmation independence is operator discipline, not
    a system-level guarantee.} SignalDoc.csv never enters the pipeline
    programmatically (hard boundary), but the human doing manual
    confirmation has necessarily seen C\&Z conventions -- a known, not fully
    closeable, soft boundary (see Ch.1's proactive disclosure).
  \item \textbf{The finite configuration menu understates underdetermination}
    (the disagreement band is a lower bound, not an unbiased estimate).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Data}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Single WRDS data vintage; sample coverage is not hardcoded anywhere
    in the pipeline and must be documented manually
    (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).
  \item Restricted to US common equity, monthly portfolio returns, CRSP +
    Compustat annual accounting data (\S\ref{sec:scope}).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Factor-specific}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{PS.} The BM-highest-quintile filter is unimplemented
    (declared \texttt{accepted\_unapplied}); the reported F-score result is a
    full-sample sort, not the paper's high-BM-subset sort -- the two numbers
    are not directly comparable.
  \item \textbf{fgr5yrLag.} Its inclusion is deliberate evidence of this
    framework's own limits, not merely a caveat: the paper-anchored identity
    (Equation~\eqref{eq:three-term}) has no term for a divergence caused by
    two teams pulling a retroactively-revised database (IBES) at different
    vintages, so this factor's gap is reported without a full attribution.
  \item \textbf{OScore.} The paper's asymmetric long-70\%/short-10\% split
    is not expressible by this engine's equal-quantile breakpoint menu
    (\S\ref{sec:scope}); whatever approximation was used to report OScore's
    numbers must be stated explicitly wherever they are cited
    (\S\ref{sec:results}).
  \item \textbf{Estimator restriction.} Only \texttt{portfolio\_sort} is
    supported (no Fama--MacBeth) -- the factor selection itself excludes
    papers whose headline result is a cross-sectional regression
    coefficient, a form of selection bias that must be acknowledged: the set
    of factors this framework \emph{can} study is a subset, not the
    population.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future work}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Within-agent dispersion study ($K \geq 5$ repeated extractions) --
    highest priority, directly resolves the largest overstatement risk in
    \S\ref{sec:discussion}.
  \item Q2 bridge track (C\&Z signal through this engine) -- unlocks
    signal/configuration separation and engine output calibration.
  \item Scale to the full HXZ$\cap$C\&Z intersection ($n=141$,
    \texttt{docs/step6.md} \S11) or the full SignalDoc population ($n=331$).
  \item Shapley-value attribution -- generalizes the field-level gap
    decomposition beyond the $\leq 5$-field regime where a full factorial is
    tractable.
  \item Extend engine capability (Fama--MacBeth estimator, derived-column
    universe filters, asymmetric quantile splits) -- each addition reduces
    the factor-selection bias above.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Conclusion}

The question this paper set out to answer was not whether a given factor
replicates, but why two careful, independent teams can reach opposite
verdicts on the same published text, and whether that disagreement can be
traced to specific, falsifiable causes. C1 answers this directly: the
paper-anchored three-term identity, applied to a sample of factors chosen
for their documented HXZ-versus-C\&Z divergence, attributes each factor's
gap to a signal/environment term, a configuration term, and an agent
residual, rather than reporting one opaque number. C2 shows that this
attribution is obtainable as a byproduct of a controlled, leakage-proof
agent pipeline whose own autonomy and capability boundary is measured, not
assumed. C3 is what makes both trustworthy: every quantity behind C1 and
C2 is reproducible with the language model switched off. Where this design
still falls short -- most importantly, the absence of a within-agent
dispersion study and of a signal-level bridge to C\&Z's own data -- is
stated above as a specific, resolvable limitation, not folded into the
headline claim.
