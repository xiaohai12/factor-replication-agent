\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The results are organized around three distinct empirical cases: one factor
for which the external teams agree, one whose paper-anchored comparison is
available but whose endpoints diverge, and one whose original estimand is not
comparable to the portfolio-sort output of this engine. Throughout, a reported
gap is not itself treated as evidence of a configuration effect: only a
complete factorial grid supports controlled Shapley attribution, while OAT
evidence and external comparisons retain their lower identification levels.

\subsection{RQ2: agent autonomy and capability boundary (cross-factor)}

All three factors reached executable frozen-signal tracks, so the pipeline
produced auditable outputs for the reviewed MethodSpecs. This is a completion
result, not an extraction-accuracy claim. The resolved MethodSpecs retain
their evidence status field by field: the resulting audit flags one weak field
for AssetGrowth, four for MeanRankRevGrowth, and thirteen for ShareVol. These
flags identify where human confirmation remains important; they do not
silently alter a backtest result. Because this study does not repeat
extraction from the same paper, it does not estimate within-agent sampling
variation, and the audit counts should not be interpreted as a probability
that the extracted method is correct.

The flags have two distinct meanings. An \emph{inferred} field is one for
which the paper gives an economic concept or a data source but not the native
database field or operational rule required by the engine. AssetGrowth's lone
flag is an inferred signal-input source column. MeanRankRevGrowth has an
inferred portfolio-sort mode and three inferred signal-input source columns.
ShareVol has inferred holding-period and signal-source details, alongside
three \emph{unspecified} portfolio-sort fields: the breakpoint basis, group
type, and sorting mode. ``Unspecified'' means that the paper itself does not
state the required implementation choice. The distinction matters: inference
is a documented mapping decision that can be checked against the source,
whereas an unspecified field requires a reviewer to supply a convention or
accept the engine default.

Figure~\ref{fig:assetgrowth-audit-trace} provides a representative audit
trace rather than reproducing every artifact for every factor. It links the
paper-evidence-backed MethodSpec from Steps~1--2, the frozen
\texttt{compute\_signal} formula generated in Step~3, the Step~4 validation
record, and the Step~5 executable-track metrics. The complete MethodSpecs,
source quotations, generated scripts, and realized signal series remain
versioned run artifacts; showing them in full in the results chapter would
obscure the empirical comparisons rather than make them more auditable.

\subsection{Per-factor results}

\subsubsection{AssetGrowth: a clean raw-return reproduction with material
configuration sensitivity}

AssetGrowth \citep{CooperGulenSchill2008} is the sample's clean reproduction
case once the comparison metric is aligned. We use the paper's value-weighted
raw monthly long--short return as the primary benchmark: $1.05\%$ per month
with $t=5.04$. The paper's separately reported value-weighted FF3 alpha of
$0.70\%$ per month ($t=3.84$) is retained as a supplementary result, but is
not mixed with raw return spreads in the replication comparison.

Under the reviewed paper configuration, the agent baseline produces
$0.93\%$ per month with $t=4.12$. Its magnitude is therefore $0.89$ times
the paper estimate, with the same sign and the same significance verdict;
the deterministic bundle classifies it as a clean reproduction. The two
external implementations reach the same qualitative conclusion. C\&Z
reports $1.73\%$ per month ($t=8.45$), and running the frozen agent signal
under the C\&Z configuration produces $1.54\%$ ($t=7.11$). The persisted HXZ
reference, evaluated over the paper window, is $0.61\%$ ($t=3.20$), while
the standardized-HXZ agent track produces $0.71\%$ ($t=3.60$). Both
matched-configuration comparisons are classified as reproduced.

The endpoint changes are nevertheless economically material. Relative to
the baseline, the C\&Z configuration increases the agent return by
$0.61\%$ per month and raises its $t$-statistic from $4.12$ to $7.11$.
The standardized HXZ configuration reduces the return by $0.22\%$ per month
and lowers the $t$-statistic to $3.60$, although the return remains positive
and statistically significant. Thus, agreement in sign and significance
does not imply invariance to portfolio design. Figure~\ref{fig:assetgrowth-mean-tstat}
locates every executed configuration alongside the paper, C\&Z, and HXZ
endpoints, making the economically material dispersion visible. The figure
is descriptive; inference on a configuration contrast comes from the paired
and joint tests below.

The paper-anchored identities place these controlled contrasts inside the
larger external gaps. C\&Z exceeds the paper by $0.68\%$ per month: the
configuration term is $+0.61\%$, the signal-and-environment term is
$+0.19\%$, and the agent replication residual is $-0.12\%$. HXZ is
$0.44\%$ below the paper: the corresponding terms are $-0.22\%$,
$-0.10\%$, and $-0.12\%$. These are exact accounting identities, but only
the configuration term is a within-engine controlled contrast; the
cross-source remainder still absorbs signal, data-vintage, and infrastructure
differences. The two AssetGrowth rows are collected in
Table~\ref{tab:three-term-identities}; Figure~\ref{fig:assetgrowth-three-term}
gives the same identities a graphical form, separating the two external
lines without treating the cross-source terms as controlled effects.

Both configuration lines admit complete factorial grids. On the C\&Z line,
the controlled Shapley decomposition assigns $+0.71\%$ per month to changing
portfolio weighting from value to equal weights. Replacing the baseline
universe---stocks on the three major US exchanges excluding financial
firms---with C\&Z's universe of common stocks listed on those exchanges,
which does not separately exclude financial firms, contributes $-0.10\%$.
The two changes sum exactly to the $+0.61\%$ configuration effect. On the HXZ
line, changing the breakpoints from the
full sample to NYSE stocks contributes $-0.17\%$, and adding the positive
common-equity requirement ($ceq>0$) to the baseline universe contributes
$-0.04\%$; together they match the $-0.22\%$ endpoint change. The C\&Z
joint test rejects that both single-switch contrasts are zero ($p=0.003$),
providing joint statistical support for its attribution. The HXZ joint test
does not reject that null ($p=0.400$). This does not test whether the final
$-0.22\%$ endpoint difference is zero; rather, it means that the two
single-switch contrasts lack joint statistical support. The HXZ Shapley
allocation should therefore be read as a controlled description of the
in-sample contrast, not as a precisely estimated switch-level attribution.
Figure~\ref{fig:assetgrowth-shapley} places the two controlled allocations in
one view: it makes the C\&Z weighting contribution visually salient while
retaining the HXZ line's weaker evidential status.

\subsubsection{MeanRankRevGrowth: a residual-dominated paper comparison}

MeanRankRevGrowth \citep{LakonishokShleiferVishny1994} is the
residual-dominated case. Table~\ref{tab:meanrank-endpoints} displays the
paper's reported value-decile return of $2.20\%$ per month beside the
engine's long--short tracks; because these endpoints do not share a common
estimator or data vintage, the paper-anchored comparison is observational.
The reviewed baseline produces $0.48\%$ per month ($t=3.00$). Under the
C\&Z configuration, the frozen signal produces $0.38\%$ ($t=2.83$), versus
the C\&Z reference of $0.55\%$ ($t=3.94$); this matched-configuration
comparison is classified as reproduced. Under the standardized HXZ
configuration, the agent produces $0.25\%$ ($t=1.33$), whereas the HXZ
reference is $-0.19\%$ ($t=1.08$). The signs differ, but neither is
statistically significant, so that comparison is inconclusive rather than a
claimed reproduction or failure.
Thus, the reproduced C\&Z comparison establishes agreement with C\&Z under
its matched configuration; it does not eliminate the much larger,
observational gap between the agent baseline and the paper endpoint.

The paper-anchored identities show why configuration is not the central
explanation. Each identity follows the path from the paper endpoint to the
agent baseline, then to the frozen agent signal under the external
configuration, and finally to the external reference. The total gaps to C\&Z
and HXZ are $-1.65\%$ and $-2.39\%$ per month, respectively, while the agent
replication residual is $-1.72\%$ on both lines. The configuration terms are
much smaller: $-0.10\%$ on the C\&Z line and $-0.23\%$ on the HXZ line. The
residual is the difference between the agent's $0.48\%$ baseline return and
the paper endpoint; it does not itself identify which implementation choice
caused that difference. The reviewed
MethodSpec retains four fields requiring human attention: the portfolio-sort
mode and three signal-input source columns are inferred. Moreover, the paper
does not specify an accounting lag or missing-return treatment, so the engine
uses its documented six-month lag and drop-missing defaults. Those choices
alter which firms, months, and accounting inputs enter the sort. The paper's
post-signal rank transform is also recorded as unapplied because the fixed
menu cannot express it. Thus, the residual combines signal-source mapping,
the omitted rank transform, documented defaults, data vintage, and engine
implementation differences. No counterfactual tracks isolate these components,
so the residual cannot be reliably allocated among them. The two
MeanRankRevGrowth rows in Table~\ref{tab:three-term-identities} make this
residual dominance explicit.

Both endpoint lines nevertheless admit complete factorial grids. On the C\&Z
line, changing deciles to quintiles contributes $-0.12\%$ per month, while
the common-stock universe change offsets this by $+0.02\%$. On the HXZ line,
moving to NYSE breakpoints, changing the stock universe, and changing from
equal to value weights contribute $-0.08\%$, $-0.09\%$, and $-0.05\%$,
respectively; together they match the $-0.23\%$ configuration effect. These
are controlled Shapley descriptions of the engine's own contrasts. However,
the corresponding joint tests do not reject that all single-switch contrasts
are zero (C\&Z $p=0.121$; HXZ $p=0.319$), so neither line supports a precise
statistical attribution to individual configuration switches.

\subsubsection{ShareVol: an intentionally non-comparable estimand}

ShareVol \citep{DatarNaikRadcliffe1998} is the boundary case. The paper's
headline result is a Fama--MacBeth coefficient ($t=-8.86$), whereas the
engine and the external references report portfolio-sort long--short spreads.
The system therefore correctly marks both paper-anchored three-term identities
as unidentified rather than subtracting incomparable quantities. This is a
substantive scope boundary, not a missing number to be filled by
approximation. Accordingly, the two ShareVol rows in
Table~\ref{tab:three-term-identities} are marked unidentified rather than
assigned numerical gap components.

The portfolio-sort endpoints remain informative on their own terms.
Table~\ref{tab:sharevol-endpoints} shows that the agent baseline is close to
zero ($0.01\%$ per month, $t=0.03$). The C\&Z configuration raises it to
$0.19\%$ ($t=0.89$), still far below the C\&Z reference of $0.91\%$
($t=3.87$); this matched-configuration comparison is not reproduced. The
result is a failure to reproduce the C\&Z portfolio-sort reference, not a
numerical verdict on the paper's regression coefficient. The
standardized-HXZ track remains close to zero ($0.01\%$, $t=0.03$), while the
HXZ reference is $-0.11\%$ ($t=0.46$). Since both are insignificant, that
comparison is inconclusive despite the sign difference.

The HXZ contrast simultaneously changes the breakpoint rule, accounting lag,
rebalancing frequency, and stock universe; the C\&Z contrast additionally
changes the number of portfolios and the weighting rule. These four and five
fields, respectively, exceed the factorial budget. The controller therefore
runs one-at-a-time ablations rather than a complete factorial grid, and no
controlled Shapley attribution is available. Across the four standardized-side
ablations, the in-sample $t$-statistic range is $0.79$ and the return changes
sign once, but no track crosses the significance threshold. This is harmonized
evidence that the portfolio-sort output is sensitive to implementation
choices, not an additive decomposition of an endpoint gap or an attribution to
any individual switch. The paper's weighting instruction cannot be expressed
as either equal or value weighting in the fixed menu, so the baseline uses the
documented value-weighting default. This further limits any paper-faithful
interpretation.

\subsection{What the cross-factor comparison establishes}

The three cases do not support a single universal cause of replication
disagreement. AssetGrowth's C\&Z-side configuration difference is dominated
by weighting; MeanRankRevGrowth's controlled configuration effects are small
relative to its paper-comparison residual; and ShareVol cannot support the
same decomposition because its paper estimand differs and its field count
exceeds the factorial budget. The common finding is methodological: the
pipeline distinguishes controlled configuration evidence from external or
underidentified residuals instead of assigning every observed difference to a
single label.

Table~\ref{tab:three-term-identities} places the available paper-anchored
identities on one common display and marks ShareVol as unidentified;
Table~\ref{tab:cross-factor-endpoints} provides the corresponding compact
endpoint comparison, while Appendix~E supplies the complete executed track
grids. The factor-specific endpoint tables above retain the reported return
and alpha values.

These conclusions are bounded by the controlled design. The fixed
configuration menu cannot express every paper-specific convention, and the
factorial budget prevents exact interaction attribution when many settings
differ. The C\&Z and HXZ references are independent comparison points rather
than ground truth or the original authors' code; differences that remain
across sources therefore cannot be assigned uniquely to the signal, data
vintage, or infrastructure. The pipeline is consequently strongest as an
auditable system for identifying estimand mismatches and measuring controlled
within-engine configuration contrasts, not as proof that it has recovered the
authors' original implementation or as a general estimate of LLM extraction
accuracy.

\subsection{$t$-channel and post-publication evidence}

For AssetGrowth and MeanRankRevGrowth, eligible positive-return comparisons
permit the $t$-channel decomposition of Section~\ref{sec:design}. This
diagnostic uses the same paper-window in-sample metrics as the endpoint
comparisons in Section~7.2. It accounts for a change in the absolute
$t$-statistic through changes in mean return, return volatility, and the
number of monthly observations; it is not a causal attribution.

For AssetGrowth, the C\&Z configuration raises the in-sample mean return from
$0.93\%$ to $1.54\%$ per month and lowers implied volatility from $4.68\%$ to
$4.49\%$, with both tracks covering 432 months. Consequently, $t$ rises from
$4.12$ to $7.11$: the higher mean is the dominant channel and the lower
volatility reinforces it. The standardized-HXZ configuration instead lowers
the mean to $0.71\%$. Its implied volatility also falls, to $4.11\%$, but
that reduction only partly offsets the lower mean, so $t$ falls to $3.60$.

For MeanRankRevGrowth, the C\&Z configuration lowers the in-sample mean from
$0.48\%$ to $0.38\%$ per month, but a volatility reduction from $2.66\%$ to
$2.25\%$ partly offsets that loss, so $t$ falls only from $3.00$ to $2.83$.
The standardized-HXZ configuration lowers the mean to $0.25\%$ and raises
implied volatility to $3.12\%$, jointly reducing $t$ to $1.33$. ShareVol's
in-sample agent tracks are technically eligible for the log decomposition,
but its baseline mean and $t$-statistic are both close to zero. The resulting
log ratios can magnify economically small changes, so they are retained as a
diagnostic rather than interpreted as substantive evidence.

The post-publication split provides a separate descriptive check. In the
AssetGrowth paper window (1968--2003), the baseline has $t=4.12$; in the
intervening 2004--2008 window it has $t=0.08$, and in the post-publication
window beginning in 2009 it has $t=0.16$. MeanRankRevGrowth's baseline is
significant in both its paper and post-publication windows ($t=3.00$ and
$t=2.53$, respectively), while ShareVol is insignificant in both. Thus,
successful in-sample replication does not imply persistence after publication.
These separate-window statistics describe realized track histories; they are
not a time-series estimate of a decline and do not establish a causal
publication effect.

\subsection{Evidence-constrained LLM diagnosis}

Step~8 was run as an optional explanation layer for all three completed
comparison bundles. It produced 25 persisted diagnosis claims (ten for
AssetGrowth, nine for MeanRankRevGrowth, and six for ShareVol), with no
claim rejected by the evidence validator. Each accepted claim names one or
more keys from the deterministic bundle and inherits its identification level
and evidence strength. For example, the AssetGrowth proposal identifies the
weighting switch as the controlled Shapley attribution on the C\&Z line,
separately records its paired-switch and joint-test evidence, and labels
post-publication decay as observational. For ShareVol, the proposal reports
the unavailable paper-versus-track comparison as an evidence limitation rather
than inventing a comparable spread.

In these cases, this provides an illustrative, bounded analytical capability:
the model can select and organize supported diagnostic claims across a
heterogeneous evidence bundle. It is not an independent empirical result or a
validation of the model's economic reasoning. In particular, validator
acceptance establishes that a claim is evidence-supported; it does not make
that interpretation unique, best, or economically most persuasive. Numeric
statements in the rendered diagnosis are reinserted deterministically from
the bundle, and the artifacts retain the status
\texttt{llm\_assisted\_proposal}. The appropriate comparison is therefore
not ``AI versus deterministic results''--the latter are the source of
truth--but whether the proposal surfaces the same evidence, limitations, and
identification level as a human analyst. A blinded expert evaluation of that
question remains future work.
