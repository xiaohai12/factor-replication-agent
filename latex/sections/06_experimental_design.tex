\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Track grid}

For each factor, the frozen agent signal $\SA$ (\S\ref{sec:pipeline}) is
executed under three configurations -- $\Cagent$, $\Ccz$, and $\Cstd$ --
producing $\Aref$, $\Acz$, and $\Ahxz$ respectively; alongside these,
$\CZ$ and $\HXZ$ are each team's own independently measured result, and
$\Pref$ is the paper's own reported number. Figure~\ref{fig:track-grid}
separates the executable track grid from these external comparison
references. It also shows the factorial or one-at-a-time (OAT) attribution
subtracks used to identify the configuration differences between the
paper-faithful baseline and each endpoint. Only the configuration varies
across $\Aref$, $\Acz$, and $\Ahxz$ -- the code hash of $\SA$ is identical
across all three by the freeze guarantee in \S\ref{sec:pipeline} -- so any
difference among them is attributable to configuration alone, never to a
silent change in the signal.

\subsection{RQ1 design: the paper-anchored three-term identity}

$\Acz$ and $\Ahxz$ are constructed by taking the frozen $\SA$ and
re-executing it with $\Ccz$ or $\Cstd$ substituted for $\Cagent$ --
otherwise unchanged -- so that Equation~\eqref{eq:three-term}'s
configuration term, $\Acz - \Aref$ (or $\Ahxz - \Aref$), is measured
directly rather than inferred.

\subsection{Field-level gap decomposition}
Our objective is to allocate the difference between the baseline
$\Cagent$ and a target configuration ($\Ccz$ or $\Cstd$) to the individual
configuration fields that change. Let $D$ be the set of those fields, and
let $R(S)$ denote the in-sample mean return from the track that switches
exactly the fields in $S \subseteq D$ to their target values, leaving all
other fields at their baseline values. In particular, $R(\varnothing)$ is
the baseline result: no field is switched and every field retains its
$\Cagent$ value.

For example, suppose only the weighting rule $w$ and breakpoint rule $b$
differ. The full factorial grid runs four tracks: $R(\varnothing)$,
$R(\{w\})$, $R(\{b\})$, and $R(\{w,b\})$. The contribution of $w$ is the
average of its effect when switched first, $R(\{w\})-R(\varnothing)$, and
when switched after $b$, $R(\{w,b\})-R(\{b\})$; $b$ is treated
symmetrically. Thus, any effect that arises only when both fields are
switched is shared rather than assigned arbitrarily to whichever field is
considered first.

More generally, where $|D| \leq 3$, we preferentially execute the full
factorial grid and allocate the total difference by Shapley values
\citep{Shapley1953}:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gap-decomp}
  \Delta = R(D) - R(\varnothing) = \sum_{j \in D} \phi_j
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:shapley-effect}
  \phi_j = \sum_{S \subseteq D \setminus \{j\}}
  \frac{|S|!\,(|D|-|S|-1)!}{|D|!}
  \left[R(S \cup \{j\}) - R(S)\right].
\end{equation}
Here, $\phi_j$ is field $j$'s average marginal effect over every possible
switching order. The Shapley efficiency property makes
Equation~\eqref{eq:gap-decomp} exact. Interactions are not reported as
separate terms; instead, their effect is allocated across the affected
fields' Shapley values through these averaged marginal contributions.

When more than three fields differ, the number of factorial combinations
grows exponentially and exceeds the acceptable computational budget, so we
fall back to a one-at-a-time (OAT) design. OAT reports
$\delta_j = R(\{j\}) - R(\varnothing)$ for each field, but in the presence
of interactions $\sum_j \delta_j$ need not equal $\Delta$; the remaining
difference is not identified by the OAT design.

\subsection{$t$-channel decomposition}
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:t-channel}
  \log t = \log \mu - \log \sigma + \tfrac{1}{2} \log n
\end{equation}
This is an identity, not a model. For an eligible baseline--track comparison
with positive mean returns, non-zero $t$-statistics, and consistent return
and $t$-statistic signs, the three terms on the right sum exactly to
$\log t$. We recover the required volatility from the persisted mean return,
$t$-statistic, and sample size using $\sigma=\mu\sqrt{n}/t$. Unlike the mean
return in Equation~\eqref{eq:gap-decomp}, a $t$-statistic cannot be
decomposed additively across configuration fields, because it is a ratio
rather than a sum; Equation~\eqref{eq:t-channel} instead decomposes
\emph{why} a $t$-statistic changed between two tracks -- via a shift in mean
return, in volatility, or in sample size -- without requiring the mean-return
decomposition's factorial design. If either track fails these conditions
(for example, because its mean return is non-positive or its sign flips), we
mark the channel decomposition as degenerate and report only the change in
absolute $t$-statistics, rather than applying undefined logarithms.

\subsection{Identification levels and significance tiers}

Identification levels are evidence labels: they do not change any estimated
return, $t$-statistic, or Shapley value, but determine the strength of the
interpretation we permit. They are derived automatically from the experiment
design and available evidence, never hand-labeled. \emph{Controlled} evidence
is a complete factorial grid, whose Shapley attribution exactly allocates the
total mean-return difference across the switched fields; it supports
high-strength attribution claims. At the individual-track level, a comparison
is also labeled controlled when exactly one configuration or snapshot axis
differs. \emph{Harmonized} evidence is an OAT or otherwise single-switch
comparison with the remaining settings held fixed; it supports medium-strength
claims about that comparison, but does not identify interactions or guarantee
additivity. \emph{Observational} evidence is a difference that is observed
without an isolating experiment, including external published references and
raw multi-configuration differences; it supports only low-strength
descriptive claims. \emph{Unidentified} means the evidence required for an
attribution was not produced -- for example, a multi-field endpoint contrast
without the requisite factorial or OAT runs -- and likewise supports only
low-strength limitation claims. This hierarchy prevents an observed
replication gap from being reported as a controlled configuration effect when
the experiment did not isolate it.
Significance is reported at three tiers -- $|t| \geq 1.96$, $2.78$, and
$3.39$ -- matching the thresholds \citet{HouXueZhang2020} themselves use
under increasingly conservative multiple-testing corrections, rather than
a single conventional cutoff.
