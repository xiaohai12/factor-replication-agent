\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Consider the sales-growth reversal signal in
\citet{LakonishokShleiferVishny1994} -- what Chen and Zimmermann code as
$\mathit{MeanRankRevGrowth}$. \citet{HouXueZhang2020} report that under
their standardized replication protocol -- value-weighted portfolios, NYSE
breakpoints -- the signal fails to clear the conventional significance bar
($t=1.08$). \citet{ChenZimmermann2022}, implementing the same paper's own
stated method rather than a uniform external protocol, replicate it with a
$t$-statistic of 3.94, and classify it as a clear predictor. Same factor,
same underlying economic idea, the same published text to work from -- and
opposite verdicts, from two teams that are each careful, well-resourced,
and working independently.

This is not an isolated case, and it does not require picking a side. Across
several hundred overlapping anomalies, \citet{HouXueZhang2020} report
that 65.4\% fail to clear $|t| \geq 1.96$ under their uniform protocol, while
\citet{ChenZimmermann2022} report that all but a handful of the same
literature's characteristics replicate under each paper's own stated method.
These two numbers are not answers to the same question -- one is a failure
rate under an externally imposed protocol, the other is a failure rate under
paper-specific implementation choices -- so it would be a mistake to read
them as a simple contradiction. But that very fact is itself the point: the
question "does this factor replicate?" does not have a single, protocol-free
answer, because a published paper does not, in general, uniquely determine
every choice its own replication requires.

The right question is therefore not whether a factor replicates, but why two
careful, independent teams can reach opposite verdicts on the same
factor -- and whether that disagreement can be attributed to specific,
falsifiable causes (a breakpoint convention, a weighting rule, a lag
assumption) rather than treated as an unexplained clash of protocols.
Answering it requires two things: a controlled execution engine that
enumerates the menu of implementation choices a paper admits, so that gaps
between two implementations can be decomposed field by field rather than
reported as one opaque number; and an independent extraction of what the
paper itself actually says, uncontaminated by prior exposure to either
team's implementation, so that a given disagreement can be attributed to a
specific side's departure from the text rather than merely to "different
choices." This second requirement is where a controlled LLM agent enters --
not as a labor-saving substitute for a human replicator, but as an
auditable, independently-arrived-at reading of the paper -- every field it
extracts traceable to a cited quote, and arrived at without ever seeing
either team's implementation -- that can serve as a neutral referee between
two existing, human-produced implementations.

Applying this framework to three factors chosen specifically because
Hou-Xue-Zhang's and Chen-Zimmermann's own reported verdicts on them differ
in kind rather than merely in degree (\S\ref{sec:data}): sales-growth
reversal, this section's opening example, whose HXZ-versus-C\&Z conflict is
attributed to a compounding breakpoint-and-weighting effect rather than to
either choice alone; asset growth \citep{CooperGulenSchill2008}, the one
case in this sample where both teams agree the signal is significant; and
share turnover \citep{DatarNaikRadcliffe1998}, whose original test is a
firm-level Fama-MacBeth regression rather than a portfolio sort at all, so
that HXZ's and C\&Z's quantile-sort implementations are both
equally-unauthorized reconstructions of the paper's actual method, not
competing readings of a shared design. Running our controlled pipeline on
these factors, we find that even in the one case where the published
verdicts agree throughout (asset growth), the largest term in the agent's
own three-way gap decomposition against the C\&Z reference spread is not
the configuration difference between implementations but the agent's own
replication residual, which accounts for 67\% of the total gap -- more
than the configuration and signal/environment terms combined. Related
evidence holds in both disagreement cases, though of a different kind in
each. For sales-growth reversal, the agent's replication residual alone
exceeds the total observed gap in magnitude (partially offset by an
opposing configuration term) -- a residual effect layered on top of the
breakpoint-and-weighting story already given above for its configuration
side. For share turnover, the agent's own signal is not robust across the
four alternative breakpoint/weighting configurations tested against it (a
$t$-statistic range of 0.79 and one outright sign flip), indicating that
at least part of the disagreement under study is intrinsic to the signal
rather than attributable to either team's protocol. Together, both cases
show that the agent's own replication precision, not just protocol
choice, is a first-order determinant of the measured gap. Part of that
replication residual, in turn, is itself a scope limitation rather than a
reading error: this engine's fixed configuration menu cannot always
express an off-menu instruction the paper actually gives -- the same
standardization tradeoff HXZ's own protocol makes in this paper's opening
example -- a distinction \S\ref{sec:results} and \S\ref{sec:discussion}
draw out factor by factor. The remainder of this section states two
research questions
this design answers (\S\ref{sec:intro}.1), fixes the unit of evaluation
(\S\ref{sec:intro}.2), and lists the paper's contributions
(\S\ref{sec:intro}.3).

\subsection{Two research questions}

\textbf{RQ1 (attribution).} For a given factor, how much of the total gap
between an external reference ($\CZ$ or $\HXZ$) and the paper's own
reported result ($\Pref$) is attributable to a difference in signal
construction (and environment), how much to a difference in portfolio-
construction configuration, and how much to the agent's own inability to
replicate the paper exactly? \S\ref{sec:framework}'s paper-anchored
three-term identity answers this by construction rather than by inference:
each of the three terms is an exact difference between two tracks this
paper's controlled engine actually runs, not a residual assigned by
assumption, and the identity holds for every factor whose own headline
result is a portfolio-sort spread, regardless of whether $\CZ$ and $\HXZ$
agree or conflict. Where the paper's own headline is instead a regression
coefficient (share turnover, \S\ref{sec:discussion}), $\Pref$ is not a
quantity the identity's terms can be taken against at all, and that
definitional gap is reported directly rather than forced into the three
terms.

\textbf{RQ2 (capability boundary).} Producing the paper-faithful
configuration that RQ1's baseline, $\Aref$, depends on is itself a
nontrivial task for the agent system (\S\ref{sec:intro}.2): what fraction
of high-impact MethodSpec fields does it resolve without a human
correction, what fraction of runs reach an executable $\Aref$ end-to-end,
and where does human correction concentrate -- on purely technical
mismatches, or on fields where the paper leaves a genuine empirical
judgment unstated? This is reported as an independent finding (C2), not
merely as a caveat on RQ1: it costs nothing beyond what RQ1's own pipeline
already produces, since every MethodSpec field the agent resolves (or
fails to) while building $\Aref$ is logged regardless of whether it is
ever used for RQ1's decomposition (\S\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Measurement unit convention}

The unit under evaluation throughout this paper is not a bare language
model call; it is the agent system as a whole -- the LLM, the tool
interface it uses to query paper text and prior evidence, the Step 2
review gate, the Step 4 sandbox validator, and the human confirmation step
that resolves any field the reviewer flags (\S\ref{sec:pipeline}). Human
confirmation is a designed component of this system, not an external
intervention that spoils the measurement: a real deployment of this
pipeline always includes a human confirming ambiguous fields before
$\Aref$ is built, so excluding that step from the unit of evaluation would
understate, not idealize, what the deployed system actually does.
Consequently, RQ2's autonomy footprint (\S\ref{sec:intro}.1) is measured
as \emph{where the agent's own designed checkpoints trigger} -- which
fields reach the reviewer, which of those the reviewer resolves without
escalating to a human, and which require a human correction -- rather than
as an estimate of raw LLM reading accuracy against some hypothetical
unsupervised baseline. This distinction matters for how the capability
results in \S\ref{sec:results} should be read: a high rate of human
correction on a given field is evidence that this system's checkpoints are
doing their job, not, by itself, evidence that the underlying model reads
the paper poorly.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C1 (RQ1, empirical, main thread).} For each of three factors,
    decompose the total gap between $\CZ$ (or $\HXZ$) and $\Pref$ into a
    signal/environment term, a configuration term, and an agent-residual
    term via three controlled counterfactual tracks
    ($\Aref$, $\Acz$, $\Ahxz$) -- an exact identity.
  \item \textbf{C2 (RQ2, agent capability, independent finding).} Report the
    agent's autonomy footprint and capability boundary across the same
    three factors, as a free byproduct of running RQ1 -- no separate annotation
    corpus required.
  \item \textbf{C3 (methodological foundation for both).} A controlled,
    auditable, LLM-assisted-but-not-LLM-controlled replication pipeline --
    every extracted field carries a cited quote and source location, the
    extraction step is leakage-proof by construction (\S\ref{sec:framework}),
    and every empirical number is deterministically reproducible with the
    LLM switched off.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Roadmap}

\S\ref{sec:related} situates this paper against the existing
replication-crisis literature and the specific HXZ-versus-C\&Z divergence
it takes as its starting point. \S\ref{sec:framework} formalizes the
notation used throughout and states the two identities the rest of the
paper is built to measure: the paper-anchored three-term identity (RQ1)
and the disagreement-band lower bound on $\ImplSpace{P}$.
\S\ref{sec:pipeline} describes the controlled pipeline itself, step by
step, including the LLM-assisted-but-not-LLM-controlled boundary that
makes C3 hold. \S\ref{sec:data} covers data sources, the scope and
assumptions that make the implementation menu enumerable at all, and the
resulting three-factor sample. \S\ref{sec:design} lays out the
experimental design -- the counterfactual tracks and ablations each factor
is run through -- that produces the numbers \S\ref{sec:results} reports
for RQ1 and RQ2. \S\ref{sec:discussion} states this design's limitations
honestly, including the leakage-proof boundary's remaining dependence on
human discipline (\S\ref{sec:framework}), and concludes.
